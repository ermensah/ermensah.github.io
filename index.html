Edouard Mensah

Applied Economist · Ph.D., University of Georgia

I study how households, firms, and markets respond to environmental risk and new technologies, and how better measurement changes what we learn about them. My work combines causal inference, field experiments, large administrative and scanner data, and machine learning, with applications to environmental health and climate adaptation, disaster-driven markets, technology adoption, and population well-being in the United States and sub-Saharan Africa.

I am currently an economist in the e-commerce and technology sector. Before that, I was a postdoctoral scholar and instructor at Penn State University and worked on impact evaluations with the World Bank's Africa Gender Innovation Lab and institutional innovations with the International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics (ICRISAT).

Working papers

Is Air Conditioning Worth More Where It Is Hotter? Humidity, Heat, and U.S. Home Prices
with I. Sue Wing and D. H. Wrenn · 2026
Paper
Abstract

Using 25.6 million single-family home sales in 36 states (2001–2019) linked to parcel-level daily temperature and humidity, this paper measures what U.S. homebuyers pay for air conditioning, the main private technology for adapting to heat. Within 1-km neighborhoods, AC raises sale prices by about 8.6% ($13,700). Holding humidity fixed, the premium falls rather than rises with the frequency of extreme heat, and it is close to zero in hot, dry places, pointing to humidity as a key dimension of heat stress omitted from temperature-only projections of cooling demand. Incomplete coding of AC in public assessor records also systematically distorts measured adoption across climates.

Is the Price Right? Evidence on Price Gouging with Application to Hurricane Harvey
with C. E. Landry and T. A. Smith · 2026
Paper
Abstract

How much should prices change when a hurricane hits, given how demand changes, and how much did they change during Hurricane Harvey? Using a model of optimal pricing and retail scanner data, we estimate the price changes justified by disaster-driven shifts in consumer demand. Prices of toilet paper, milk and bottled water generally changed very little (typically under 12%) and were mostly consistent with demand-driven market behavior, suggesting little evidence of excessive pricing. Many state anti-price-gouging caps may be too rigid because they can block economically justified price adjustments.

Who Answers the Survey? Experimental Evidence on Respondent Selection and Nonclassical Measurement Error
with A. D. Jones, A. Dillon and J. W. Somé · 2026
Paper
Abstract

In 1,028 farm households in Burkina Faso, we randomly assigned who reported farm activities (the household head, a random adult, or each plot manager). Proxy reports create an inverse farm size–productivity relationship, cut the labor elasticity of output by three quarters, and reverse the policy conclusion on child diets. We decompose the distortions into attenuation and outcome-correlated error, propose a diagnostic toolkit and practitioner checklist, and test a cross-fitted two-sample machine learning prediction correction.

Predicting Poverty-Resilient Households Using Machine Learning in Sub-Saharan Africa
with N. S. Sayouti · Oxford Bulletin of Economics and StatisticsForthcoming
Paper
Abstract

We forecast household resilience to poverty by estimating each household's conditional well-being distribution with a mixture density network that combines a Gaussian mixture model with a neural network. Resilience is the probability of remaining above a poverty line. Using three waves of nationally representative panel data from Nigeria, we compare this approach to two-step conditional moments, elastic net, single-Gaussian neural networks, gradient boosting and tree-based mixture models under temporal and spatial out-of-sample validation. The mixture density network forecasts well-being distributions most accurately, reducing bias in resilience scores and their policy implications.
